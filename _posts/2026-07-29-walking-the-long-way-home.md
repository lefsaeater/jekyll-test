The shortest route home takes twelve minutes. The better route takes twenty-seven.

It crosses behind the library, follows a row of old brick buildings, and cuts through a park where the paths seem to have been placed according to an argument nobody remembers. Nothing remarkable happens there. That is part of its value.

## The scale of noticing

Moving quickly makes a place legible in broad categories: crossing, shop, tree, traffic. Walking without urgency reveals a finer vocabulary. One tree is dropping leaves weeks before the others. A repair in the pavement traces the shape of something that used to stand there. The bakery vents warm air into the same narrow alley every afternoon.

These details do not demand interpretation. They simply make the world feel less interchangeable.

Photography helps, though I try not to turn every observation into a collection task. Sometimes taking a picture sharpens attention. Sometimes the phone becomes another pane of glass between the moment and me. Knowing the difference is a practice.

## Useful inefficiency

Optimization is powerful when the destination is clear. Many parts of life are not improved by arriving sooner. A conversation, a meal, a book, and a walk all lose something when efficiency becomes their organizing principle.

The longer route creates a transition between work and home. Problems rearrange themselves without being actively solved. A sentence occasionally arrives fully formed. More often, I simply return with enough distance to see the day at its proper size.

There is no dramatic lesson hiding at the end of the path. Only a reminder that time can be used without being extracted, and that familiar ground still contains more than we have noticed.
